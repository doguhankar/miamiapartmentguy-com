In Miami, a rental listing that looks identical on the surface can put you in two very different situations: an individually owned condo unit, where you lease from the owner, or a professionally managed apartment, where you lease from the building operator. This applies citywide; Edgewater specifically has a large share of individually owned condo inventory, so if that's where you're searching, our [Edgewater-focused version](/blog/best-edgewater-condos-to-rent) walks through the same questions with that market in mind.

## Who you're actually leasing from

A condo unit is owned by an individual who may self-manage or use a property manager. Approval, maintenance response, and renewal are that owner's decisions, filtered through the building's HOA rules. A managed apartment building is owned and operated as a business, with set criteria applied the same way to every applicant.

## What to ask about approval

Don't assume either path is faster. Ask directly: "Who approves my application — the owner, a property manager, or the building's leasing office?" and "What's the typical turnaround once I submit everything?" Get that answer in writing rather than estimating it from the listing type.

## What to ask about lease terms and renewal

Owner-leased condos can sometimes offer more flexible lease lengths, but that depends entirely on the individual owner's plans — including whether they intend to sell or move back in. Ask: "What happens to my lease if you sell the unit?" and "Is renewal typically offered, and at what notice period?" A managed building's renewal process is usually documented in the lease; ask to see that section before signing either type.

## What to ask about maintenance

In a condo, ask who handles repairs — the owner directly, a property manager, or the HOA for building-wide systems like elevators and common-area AC. In a managed apartment, ask what the building's standard response time is for maintenance requests and whether that's written anywhere. "It's usually fast" is not an answer you can hold anyone to.

## Compare the full cost, not just the rent

A lower advertised rent on a condo doesn't automatically mean a lower total cost. Ask about HOA-related restrictions that could affect you, any owner-specific fees, and whether a security deposit or move-in requirement differs from a managed building's standard schedule. Use the same written-quote comparison you'd use for any two apartments — base rent, required fees, and deposits — rather than assuming one category is cheaper by default.

Both paths can work well. The one that fits you depends on how much you value a documented, uniform process versus the flexibility (and uncertainty) of dealing directly with an individual owner.
